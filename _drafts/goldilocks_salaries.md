# Goldilocks and Getting Public Employee Salaries Just Right

Democratic societies depend on a professional public workforce.  To do their jobs well, public servants need to be paid enough to attract competent and ethical people while avoiding excessive compensation that invites nepotism and budget problems.  The ancient fairy‑tale “Goldilocks and the Three Bears” offers an apt metaphor: a public paycheck should be neither too hot nor too cold, but *just right*.

## The Cost of Paying Too Little

When civil servants are underpaid, governments struggle to recruit and retain skilled workers.  Low wages can create incentives for staff to look elsewhere or for corrupt actors to supplement their income illicitly.  Research by anti‑corruption experts shows that low government wages reduce efficiency and productivity and encourage misuse of public resources[^1].  Economic models developed by the International Monetary Fund reinforce this point: civil servants paid below a fair wage may engage in “satisficing” behaviour, accepting bribes just to reach an acceptable income level, and societies facing low public wages may even tolerate corruption[^2].

These findings underscore the obvious: if government jobs pay far less than comparable private‑sector roles, talented people will go elsewhere and the public risks being served by those unable or unwilling to resist corruption.  Adequate pay is the first line of defence against bribery and inefficiency.

## The Pitfalls of Paying Too Much

On the other end of the spectrum, overly generous compensation breeds its own problems.  When salaries and benefits far exceed what a similar job would pay elsewhere, positions become highly coveted and are vulnerable to nepotism and cronyism.  Ethics scholars note that favouritism, cronyism and nepotism undermine fairness and transparency because jobs are awarded based on personal connections rather than merit, leading to inferior service and eroding public trust[^3]. An international policy brief warns that nepotism undermines meritocracy and significantly erodes confidence in democratic institutions[^4].  Excessive pay packages can also create rigid bureaucracies that resist change because employees are motivated to protect their privileged status rather than adapt to evolving needs.  International experience underscores these dangers. Economist Alex Tabarrok notes that in India public‑sector wages can be five to ten times higher than comparable private‑sector salaries, so lucrative that millions of applicants invest years studying for entrance exams[^10].  In one case, 2.3 million people applied for just 368 menial “office boy” positions in Uttar Pradesh, and around 80 percent of the unemployed in Tamil Nadu spend their time preparing for civil‑service exams instead of working[^10]. Because government jobs promise lifetime tenure and rich perks, the opportunity cost of this rent seeking exceeds the value of the jobs themselves; Tabarrok estimates that India may waste resources equivalent to roughly 1.4 percent of GDP each year on exam preparation and other unproductive efforts[^10]. In a follow‑up post, Tabarrok reports that the social prestige attached to civil‑service jobs leads India’s most educated youth to eschew private‑sector work, producing towns where hundreds of thousands of young people cram for government exams for years[^11].  Similar patterns appear in Greece and Brazil: macroeconomic models show that dropping public‑sector wages by ten percent could raise private‑sector productivity by 3.8 percent and reduce unemployment by 7.3 percent, and that aligning public pensions with private‑sector pensions could increase aggregate output by more than 11 percent[^12]. These examples illustrate how overly generous compensation can distort labour markets, misallocate talent and depress overall economic growth.

Getting compensation wrong in either direction therefore harms institutions.  The goal is not to create a race to the bottom or to offer lavish rewards; it is to find a market‑based rate that attracts qualified staff without fuelling entitlement and waste.

## Finding the “Just Right” Zone

One practical test for salary adequacy is how difficult it is to fill vacancies.  If a government repeatedly struggles to attract qualified candidates for a role advertised widely, the pay may be too low.  If, by contrast, there is a long line of eager applicants and employee rosters show the same surnames or obvious insider connections, compensation may be too high.

Managers should treat compensation as part of a dynamic labour market. Workers move between the public and private sectors depending on opportunities; this mobility is healthy.  Governments must be flexible enough to raise salaries when certain skills are scarce and willing to hold the line or even reduce pay when roles can be filled easily.

## Tools for Controlling Salary Growth

The following mechanisms can help elected officials and administrators keep public wages in the Goldilocks zone.  Ideally, adjustments are made when the broader economy is doing well so that displaced workers can find other jobs and morale is less likely to suffer.

### Freeze Increases When Necessary

If compensation has crept far beyond comparable market rates, an across‑the‑board salary freeze may be needed to halt further drift.  A freeze is a blunt instrument and can make it harder to adjust pay for critical positions, but it buys time for more targeted reforms.

### Conduct Classification Reviews (Desk Audits)

Bureaucracies tend to inflate titles and pay over time.  A classification audit compares what workers actually do with the job descriptions on file.  The U.S. Office of Personnel Management describes a desk audit as an interview conducted by a human‑resources specialist to understand the duties and difficulty of a position[^6]. These audits can uncover situations where staff were promoted without an increase in responsibilities.  Savings from reclassification can be shared through modest bonuses to high performers while encouraging others to seek roles that better match their skills.

### Benchmark Against the Labour Market

Pay should not be set in a vacuum.  A 2015 analysis in *Governing* magazine stresses that benchmarking compensation against comparable jobs in both public and private sectors helps agencies offer competitive salaries without overpaying[^5].  Too few governments routinely benchmark, which leads to compensation “drift” and makes collective bargaining more contentious[^5]. Benchmarking every few years—ideally aligned with collective‑bargaining cycles—and publishing the results promotes transparency and helps policy makers adjust wages thoughtfully.

### Negotiate Contracts to Reduce Abuse and Future Liabilities

When negotiating collective agreements, governments should close loopholes that invite manipulation.  Overtime should be carefully controlled; paying time‑and‑a‑half is reasonable for occasional extra work, but when overtime becomes routine the base salary is likely too low or staffing levels are inadequate.  Pension design also matters: public pensions are often calculated using an employee’s highest earning years, which can encourage “pension spiking.”  A report from Washington’s Law Enforcement Officers’ and Fire Fighters Plan 2 explains that salary spiking involves manipulating compensation during the final average salary period to boost retirement benefits[^7] and cautions that such practices undermine public trust in the retirement system[^7].  Clear policies that exclude overtime and special payments from pension calculations can reduce these abuses.

### Limit Unnecessary Leave and Political Activities

Generous paid leave policies and time off for non‑work activities are popular benefits, but they must be justified.  Additional holidays or time off for campaigning should be scrutinized and benchmarked against the private sector.  The goal is to provide reasonable work–life balance without creating hidden costs.

## Paying a Premium for Public Safety

Policing and firefighting differ from most public roles: there are few private‑sector equivalents, the work is dangerous, and corruption or poor performance has immediate consequences.  For these reasons, communities generally pay a premium for these jobs.  That said, premium pay is a privilege, not a blank cheque.

### The Police Premium

Police officers face constant temptation from those who would rather pay a bribe than obey the law.  Low wages may invite corruption[^2], so the public should be willing to pay enough to attract honest, upstanding officers.  The work is also hazardous and often involves overnight shifts.  Medical research shows that night and rotating shift work disrupts the circadian rhythm and is linked to higher risks of heart disease, diabetes and other health problems[^8]. Officers should therefore be compensated for these burdens.

Premium pay should not insulate police departments from scrutiny. Excessive overtime, inflated staffing ratios (for example, too many “chiefs” compared with patrol officers) and opaque pension formulas can quickly push costs beyond what taxpayers can sustain.  It is also important to invest in technology and crime‑prevention strategies that reduce the need for personnel.  For example, San Francisco’s real‑time investigation centre—a 24/7 hub combining CCTV, automated license‑plate readers and drones—helped cut overall crime by 20 percent and car thefts by 42 percent in 2025[^9].  Reallocating salary savings from unfilled positions to such technology programs can allow agencies to do more with fewer officers.

### The Fire Premium

Firefighters perform physically demanding and dangerous work and, like police officers, often work overnight shifts.  Many of the health effects of shift work documented for other professions apply here as well[^8].  Technologies such as modern sprinkler systems, fire‑resistant materials and early warning sensors have significantly reduced fire deaths and property damage over time. Compensation models should reflect these changing realities: pay enough to ensure that fire departments can recruit and retain skilled professionals, but adjust staffing levels and specialties as preventive technologies reduce demand.  Regular benchmarking and transparent overtime policies are critical.

## Nepotism: A Persistent Threat

Few issues destroy morale and public confidence faster than nepotism. Awarding jobs or contracts to relatives and friends erodes meritocracy and undermines institutional integrity.  Ethics experts emphasise that favouritism and nepotism interfere with fairness because they grant undue advantage to those who may not have the best credentials[^3]. The INADIS policy brief similarly notes that nepotism erodes public trust and concentrates power among privileged groups[^4].

There may be rare circumstances where a civic‑minded individual from an engaged public‑service family takes a substantial pay cut to contribute to their community.  Such cases should be transparent and justifiable.  However, the default should be strict anti‑nepotism rules and an open, competitive hiring process.  Managers asked to oversee a mayor’s nephew or mistress will inevitably find it difficult to hold that person accountable, damaging morale and public faith.  Vigilance against nepotism is a necessary complement to getting public salaries “just right.”

## Conclusion

Public employee compensation is a balancing act.  Pay too little and governments cannot recruit the talent they need, leading to poor performance and corruption.  Pay too much and nepotism, bloated bureaucracies and unfunded pension liabilities will follow.  By benchmarking salaries, conducting regular classification audits, negotiating contracts that limit abuse and basing premiums for police and fire on transparent criteria, communities can ensure they are getting the competent public workforce they deserve without wasting taxpayer money.  That’s the Goldilocks principle applied to government: not too hot, not too cold—just right.
